**Title:** PSA from someone who ran Level 1 through a cold winter: you probably don't need a panel upgrade for Level 2

In the winter of 2023 I spent four months charging an Ioniq 5 off a regular 120V garage outlet. I'd assumed anything better meant a panel upgrade. On a 20 degree January night it added about 30 miles in eleven hours. A cold battery takes a big chunk of what a Level 1 cord delivers, so the number on paper and the number in your driveway are not the same.

When I finally got a proper quote, I learned that most homes don't need a panel upgrade for a 40 amp charger. It needs a dedicated 50 amp circuit (the 80% continuous load rule), and a real load calculation on a typical 200 amp service often shows the room is already there. If the math is tight, a load management device or a charger turned down to 32 amps costs far less than a new panel.

My rough rule: if you drive under about 40 miles a day and your winters are mild, Level 1 can work. If you drive more than that, or it gets cold where you live, 40 amps covers almost everyone overnight.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Do I need a Level 2 charger at home for my EV, or is a regular 120-volt outlet enough?

Short answer: if you reliably drive under about 40 miles a day and your winters are mild, a regular 120-volt outlet (Level 1) can be enough. For almost everyone else, a 40-amp Level 2 charger is the right home setup, and it usually does not need a panel upgrade.

I'm an automotive engineer, and I tested this the hard way. In the winter of 2023 I charged a Hyundai Ioniq 5 from a 120-volt garage outlet for four months. On a 20-degree January night it added roughly 30 miles in eleven hours. Some of what a Level 1 cord delivers goes into warming a cold battery, so winter results fall well short of the spec sheet. One long day followed by a cold night and you start the morning behind.

A 40-amp Level 2 charger at 240 volts delivers about 9.6 kW, which is roughly 25 to 30 miles of range per hour on most EVs. A typical commute is back in an hour or two, and even a long road day refills overnight.

The electrical side is where people overspend. Charging counts as a continuous load, so a 40-amp charger needs a dedicated 50-amp circuit. That sounds like it means a panel upgrade, but often it doesn't. Ask your electrician for a load calculation rather than a guess, because many 200-amp services already have room. If yours is tight, a load management device or setting the charger to 32 amps costs far less than replacing the panel.

If you stay on Level 1 for now, make sure the outlet isn't shared with other garage loads. A shared circuit is a common cause of tripped breakers and slow sessions.

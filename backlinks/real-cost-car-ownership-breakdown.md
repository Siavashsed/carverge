## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is the real cost of owning a car, and is depreciation really bigger than fuel?

For most people buying a newer vehicle, yes. Depreciation is usually the largest single cost of ownership, and it's also the one almost nobody budgets for because no bill ever arrives.

AAA's 2025 Your Driving Costs study put the average cost of a new vehicle at roughly $12,300 a year. That figure bundles the things you'd expect: fuel, insurance, maintenance and repairs, tires, registration and taxes, and financing. But the biggest slice is typically the value the car loses simply by getting older and accumulating miles.

Here's how I think about it as an automotive engineer.

First, the drop is front-loaded. A new car loses the most value in its first three years, which is why a lightly used one often costs far less per year to own, even if the sticker price looks similar to a discounted new model.

Second, fuel is visible and depreciation isn't. You feel every fill-up, so you optimize for mpg. But a car that gets 3 mpg better and loses an extra $1,500 a year in value is not the cheaper car.

Third, model choice matters. Some vehicles hold their value far better than others, and checking resale data before you buy can save you thousands.

Fourth, time is your friend. Once the loan is paid off and the steepest depreciation is behind you, the cost per year of a well-maintained car drops sharply.

So when you budget, add it all up: fuel, insurance, maintenance, tires, taxes, financing, and the estimated loss in value over the years you plan to keep the car. Divide by years owned. That's your real number.

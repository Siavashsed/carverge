## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do you properly inspect a used car before buying it?

Think of it as two separate jobs. Skip either one and you're guessing.

Job one is your own check, about 45 minutes. Do it in daylight, with the engine cold. Walk around the car and look at panel gaps, paint that doesn't match, and uneven tire wear, which can point to alignment or suspension problems. Lift the oil cap and look for milky residue or heavy sludge. Check the fluids, then open the trunk and lift the carpet to look for rust or water. Inside, test every switch, window, and the A/C. On the test drive, listen for noises on a cold start, then drive the car on city streets and at highway speed. Brake firmly once when it's safe. Watch for pulling, vibration, hesitation, and warning lights.

Job two is a paid pre-purchase inspection (PPI) at an independent shop the seller didn't pick. Expect to pay roughly $150. A mechanic can put the car on a lift and look for leaks, worn bushings, accident repair, and rust. They can also scan for stored fault codes. This is the part you can't do in a parking lot.

A few rules I follow. Never use the seller's recommended mechanic. Don't let a binder of service receipts replace an inspection, since receipts show what was done and not what condition the car is in now. And if the seller refuses an independent inspection, walk away.

I learned this the hard way in 2011 on an Audi A4. It looked clean and had a thick receipt binder, and I never lifted the oil cap. The problem showed up three weeks later.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What maintenance does my car actually need, and which dealer service recommendations can I safely skip?

The short answer: follow the maintenance schedule in your owner's manual, not the menu the service advisor hands you. They're two different documents, and the difference can run a few hundred dollars per visit.

The manual schedule is written by the engineers who designed and validated your vehicle. It lists what the car needs at each mileage interval: typically oil and filter changes, tire rotations, cabin and engine air filters, brake fluid on a time basis, and inspections. Many modern cars also specify transmission fluid, coolant and spark plugs, but at long intervals.

The shop's menu is often a different animal. It's built around what's profitable and easy to sell, so you'll commonly see fuel system cleanings, throttle body cleanings, engine flushes, power steering flushes and 'preventive' fluid exchanges that the manufacturer never asked for. Some are harmless, some are simply unnecessary, and a few, like aggressive flushes on high-mileage transmissions, can do more harm than good.

Here's how I handle it. First, open the manual to the maintenance section before you book. Second, when you get a quote, ask the advisor to show you where each item appears in the manufacturer's schedule. If it isn't there, ask why it's being recommended for your specific car. Third, decline anything that can't be justified, and revisit it only if you have a real symptom.

There are legitimate exceptions. If most of your driving is short trips, towing, dusty roads or extreme temperatures, your manual usually has a 'severe service' schedule with shorter intervals. That's the one to follow, and it's printed right there in the same book.

Keep your receipts, stay within the manual's intervals, and you'll protect both the car and your warranty without overpaying.

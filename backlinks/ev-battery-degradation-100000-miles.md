## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How much battery capacity does an electric car lose after 100,000 miles?

Less than most people assume. Most EV batteries that pass 100,000 miles still hold roughly 85 to 90 percent of their original capacity. The bigger surprise is when that loss happens: most of it comes in the first two or three years, and then the curve flattens out.

I'm an automotive engineer and I've measured this on real cars. Last March I connected an OBDLink MX+ to a reader's 2018 Model 3 Long Range with 112,400 miles on it and ran Scan My Tesla early one morning in a grocery store parking lot. It reported 68.9 kWh of usable capacity, roughly 90 percent of what the pack held new. That is a six-year-old car with well over 100,000 miles, and it still has most of its range.

A few things shape how a pack ages:

Heat. Batteries that spend years in hot climates without good thermal management tend to degrade faster.

Charging habits. Leaving the car at 100 percent charge for long stretches and relying heavily on DC fast charging both add wear over time. Daily charging to around 80 percent is gentler.

Chemistry and thermal design. Packs with active liquid cooling generally hold up better than older passively cooled designs.

If you're buying a used EV, don't judge battery health by the range estimate on the dash. That number moves with temperature, driving style and recent trips. Get an actual capacity reading with an OBD tool and an app like Scan My Tesla, or ask a dealer for a battery health report.

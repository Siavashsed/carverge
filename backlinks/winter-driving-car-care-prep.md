## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Are winter tires better than all-wheel drive for driving in snow and ice?

In most real winter conditions, yes. Winter tires do more for you than all-wheel drive, and the reason comes down to what each one actually does.

AWD distributes engine power to all four wheels. That helps when you're accelerating from a stop or climbing a slippery grade, because there are more driven wheels sharing the work. But driving is more than going. You also have to stop and turn, and AWD adds nothing to either. Your brakes act on all four wheels in any car, and cornering grip depends entirely on the tires' contact with the road.

Tires are the only part of the car that touches the surface. Winter tires use a softer rubber compound that stays flexible well below about 45°F, where all-season rubber begins to harden and lose grip. They also have deeper treads and thousands of small slits, called sipes, that bite into snow and ice. The result is shorter braking distances and better steering response, which is where most winter accidents actually happen.

I learned this myself in January 2024 on a 6 percent hill near Stowe, Vermont, at 12°F. My AWD wagon, wearing worn all-seasons, climbed the hill easily. Then I braked at the top and slid about 40 feet backward into a snowbank. The drivetrain got me up the hill. The tires couldn't get me stopped.

My practical advice: if you live somewhere with regular snow or sustained cold, buy a set of four winter tires before you consider paying extra for AWD. A front-wheel-drive car on four winter tires will often outperform an AWD car on all-seasons. Ideally, you do both, but if you have to choose, choose tires. Always fit all four, not just two, so the car stays balanced.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What is NHTSA's automatic emergency braking rule, and when will it be required on all new cars?

In April 2024, NHTSA finalized a federal rule requiring automatic emergency braking (AEB) on every new passenger car and light truck sold in the US. The compliance deadline is September 2029, so you'll see it become universal over the next few model years, and many automakers already fit it voluntarily.

The key requirements are:

1. Forward collision avoidance. The car must automatically brake to avoid contact with a vehicle ahead at speeds up to 62 mph. Earlier voluntary commitments were much looser, and many systems only reduced impact speed.

2. Speed-reduction performance. The system must also work when the lead vehicle is slower or stopped, not just in a simple stationary-target test.

3. Pedestrian detection. The rule includes detecting pedestrians, including in low-light conditions, which is a major challenge for cameras and radar.

4. Always on. The system has to be active by default each time you start the car.

Why is 62 mph so hard? Stopping distance grows with the square of speed. A car at 62 mph carries about six times the kinetic energy it does at 25 mph. The sensors have to detect the obstacle farther away, the software has to decide quickly and confidently, and the brakes and tires have to deliver near-maximum deceleration without a human pressing the pedal. The trade-off is false positives: a system tuned too aggressively will brake for shadows or overpasses, which drives drivers to switch it off.

I got a taste of this on a closed lot with a borrowed crossover and a foam target, and the gap between slowing down and actually stopping is very noticeable.

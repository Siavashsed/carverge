Winter Kills Your Battery First, So Start Your Car Prep There

An automotive engineer's plan to prepare your car for winter: battery testing, coolant, oil, tires and EV range, in the order that prevents breakdowns.

JC
James Carr November 17, 2026 · 5 min read

Most winter breakdowns aren't dramatic. Usually it's a tired battery meeting its first 5°F morning, and you can prevent nearly all of those with a free load test and about an hour of work in October. I learned this the humbling way in January 2019. I was standing in a Detroit parking structure at 6:40 a.m. while my own four-year-old Accord answered the key with a single click. Two months earlier that battery had tested at 410 cold cranking amps against a 550 rating, and I'd told myself it would make one more season. I had spent the week before writing about cold-start chemistry.

After years of looking at failed parts and answering reader emails, here's where I land: winter prep is a battery problem first and a tire problem second. Everything else comes a distant third. The ice scraper and the blanket in the trunk matter, but you only reach for them after something else has already failed. EV owners have a slightly different order, and I'll get to that.

Test the Battery Before the First Hard Freeze

Cold hits a lead-acid battery from both sides. The chemical reaction that makes current slows down, and the oil thickens, so the starter wants more amps at the exact moment the battery can give fewer. AAA puts the loss at roughly 35 percent of a battery's strength at 32°F and up to 60 percent at 0°F, and starting the engine in that cold can take up to twice the power. And here's the thing... the battery that dies in January was usually weak in October. You just couldn't hear it yet.

What the test actually tells you

AutoZone, O'Reilly and Advance will all run a conductance test for free. Ask for the printout. If measured CCA comes in under about 80 percent of the number on the label, replace the battery now. Don't rely on resting voltage. I've had batteries read a perfect 12.6 volts on a multimeter and then collapse under a 200-amp cranking load.

Age is the other tell. In hot climates three years is a long life, and up north four to five is typical. Clean the terminals while you're there, since a crust of white corrosion adds enough resistance to make a healthy battery act dead. I've watched a $3 wire brush "fix" a no-start that the owner had already budgeted $220 for.

Oil, Coolant and Washer Fluid Fail in Different Ways

The number before the W on an oil bottle is its cold rating. A 0W oil reaches the bearings far faster at -20°F than a 10W, and most engine wear happens in the first few seconds after a cold start. Run exactly what your owner's manual calls for. Small turbocharged engines are the least forgiving here, and I've written separately about how the wrong oil quietly wears out a turbo engine.

Coolant is where I see the ugliest surprises. Skip the floating-ball tester from the gas station and spend $25 on a refractometer, which reads freeze point to within a few degrees. Protection from a proper 50/50 ethylene glycol mix runs to roughly -34°F. I've pulled samples from cars that had been topped off with plain water for years, and one read a freeze point of +10°F. That's a cracked block waiting for one cold night.

Washer fluid sounds trivial until it isn't. Summer blue fluid freezes in the lines, and then you're stuck behind a salt truck with a gray windshield and nothing to spray. Swap it for fluid rated to -20°F or lower before the first freeze, and run the washers until the new stuff comes out of the nozzles.

Tires Decide Whether You Stop, Not Your Drivetrain

Tire pressure follows the gas law, which works out to roughly 1 psi lost for every 10°F drop. Set them to 35 psi in a 70°F garage and you're near 30 on a 20°F morning, with mushier handling and a TPMS light you'll probably ignore. Check them cold, once a month. The stakes are real: Federal Highway Administration data puts 24 percent of weather-related crashes on snowy, slushy or icy pavement.

The AWD misconception

This is the one I argue about most. All-wheel drive helps you get moving. It does nothing for stopping, because every car already brakes with all four wheels. In my experience a front-drive sedan on winter tires will outstop an AWD crossover on worn all-seasons, and it isn't close. Look for the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol on the sidewall. Real winter tires run roughly $700 to $1,200 a set on steel wheels, plus somewhere to store them. If you only see snow a dozen days a year, an all-weather tire with that symbol is a fair compromise, and I won't pretend otherwise.

Okay, a confession. My brother-in-law Dave lifts his wiper arms off the glass every night snow is forecast, like two little surrender flags, and I teased him about it for three winters. Then I tore a $28 blade clean off its arm because I hit the wiper switch on a frozen windshield before the defroster had done anything. So... maybe Dave's onto something? I'm honestly not sure it matters for everyone, and some wiper springs don't love sitting upright all night. But I haven't laughed at him since.

EVs Rarely Strand You, but They Lose Range and Hide a Weak Link

An EV doesn't have a starter to fail, but cold still costs you. When AAA tested five EVs in 2019, average range at 20°F fell 12 percent with the climate system off and 41 percent with the cabin heater running. The second number is the one you'll actually live with. Precondition while plugged in, so the wall warms the battery and cabin instead of the pack. A heat pump makes a big difference too, which is part of why the Model Y's 2020 launch drew a line in the sand for every EV that followed.

Here's what dealers rarely mention. The small 12-volt battery still runs the computers, the door handles and the contactors that connect the main pack. When it dies, a car with an 80 percent charge won't wake up... which feels absurd the first time it happens to you. I've seen it on a Hyundai Kona and a Model 3 in the same January. Get that battery tested along with everything else. If you're still choosing what to buy, your driveway and charging setup decide more than the badge, and an EV without home charging in a Minnesota winter is a harder ownership experience than the brochure admits.

What belongs in the trunk

My kit is short, and every item has earned its spot. NHTSA's winter checklist covers the rest.

  • A lithium jump pack (a NOCO Boost GB40 runs about $100 and works without a second car)
  • A folding shovel and a bag of kitty litter for traction
  • Gloves, a wool blanket and a phone cable
  • A brush with a long handle, because you can't clear the roof of a crossover from the side

Do one thing this week: drive to a parts store and ask for a free battery test with a printout. If measured CCA comes in under 80 percent of the rating on the label, replace it now, on your schedule and at a price you chose. Otherwise you'll be buying it at 6:40 a.m. in a parking garage, the way I did.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my car battery will make it through winter?

Get a free conductance test at a parts store and compare the measured cold cranking amps to the rating on the label. Under about 80 percent means replace it before the cold arrives. A battery over four or five years old in a northern climate is living on borrowed time either way.

Do I really need winter tires if I have all-wheel drive?

AWD helps you accelerate on snow but does nothing for braking or turning, which is where crashes happen. If you get regular snow and ice, winter tires are worth more than the drivetrain. For light snow areas, an all-weather tire with the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol is a reasonable middle ground.

How much range does an electric car lose in winter?

AAA's 2019 testing found an average 41 percent range loss at 20°F with the cabin heater on, and about 12 percent with it off. Preconditioning while plugged in and having a heat pump both cut that loss noticeably. Also have the 12-volt battery tested, because when it fails the car won't start no matter how charged the main pack is.
